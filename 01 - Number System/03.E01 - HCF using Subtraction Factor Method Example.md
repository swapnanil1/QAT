# HCF using Subtraction Factor Method Example

**Problem:** A school wants to purchase identical storage boxes to store exactly 105 math books, 140 physics books, and 175 chemistry books, with each box containing books of only one subject and having the same number of books. What is the largest number of books that can be placed in each box?

(A) 5
(B) 7
(C) 35
(D) 105

## Solution:

This problem asks for the largest number of books per box such that this number exactly divides the total books for each subject. This is equivalent to finding the Highest Common Factor (HCF) or Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) of 105, 140, and 175. We can use the subtraction method or its extension (finding HCF of differences), as hinted by the parent concept [[01 - Number System/03 - HCF and LCM.md]] (Introduces methods for finding HCF, including those based on differences).

Step 1: Identify the numbers for which the HCF is required.
Numbers are 105, 140, 175.

Step 2: Use the property that `HCF(a, b, c) = HCF(a, b-a, c-b)`. Calculate the differences between the numbers.
Difference 1: 140 - 105 = 35
Difference 2: 175 - 140 = 35
Difference 3: 175 - 105 = 70

Step 3: Find the HCF of these differences (and potentially one of the original numbers, though often HCF of differences is sufficient). The HCF of the original numbers must also divide their differences.
We need to find `HCF(35, 35, 70)`.
Clearly, 35 divides 35 and 35 divides 70 (70 = 2 * 35).
Therefore, the HCF of the differences is 35.

Step 4: Verify if this HCF (35) divides all the original numbers.
   - 105 / 35 = 3 (Divisible)
   - 140 / 35 = 4 (Divisible)
   - 175 / 35 = 5 (Divisible)
Since 35 divides all original numbers and it is the HCF of their differences, it is the HCF of the original numbers.

Step 5: Conclude the answer.
The largest number of books that can be placed in each box is the HCF, which is 35.

This matches option (C).

## Shortcut/Tip

*   **HCF of Differences:** The core idea is that the HCF of a set of numbers must also divide the difference between any two numbers in the set. Finding the HCF of the differences (`HCF(b-a, c-b, c-a)`) often gives the HCF of the original numbers (`HCF(a, b, c)`) or a multiple of it. It's particularly useful when the differences are smaller than the original numbers.
*   **Simplification:** In this case, the differences were 35, 35, and 70. The HCF is clearly 35. Always check if this HCF divides the original numbers. If it does, you've found the answer. If it doesn't, the actual HCF will be a factor of the HCF you found from the differences.
*   **When to Use:** The subtraction/difference method is often faster than prime factorization when the numbers are relatively close together, leading to smaller differences. It's closely related to the [[01 - Number System/03.E02 - HCF using Euclidean Algorithm Example.md]] (Demonstrates the standard Euclidean algorithm, which is essentially repeated subtraction).
*   **Distractor Check:** Options (A) 5 and (B) 7 are common factors, but not the *highest* common factor. Option (D) 105 is one of the original numbers but is not a factor of 140 or 175. Always ensure the chosen HCF divides *all* given numbers.
