# Applying Fermat's Little Theorem Example

**Problem:**

What is the remainder when 2^1000 is divided by 13?

(A) 1
(B) 3
(C) 4
(D) 9

## Solution:

This problem involves finding the remainder of a very large power (2^1000) where the divisor (13) is a prime number. Fermat's Little Theorem, stated in [[01 - Number System/04 - Remainder Theorem.md]] (Provides theorems for remainder calculations), is ideal for this situation.

**Step 1: Check conditions for Fermat's Little Theorem.**
   The theorem states that if `p` is a prime number, then for any integer `a` not divisible by `p`, we have `a^(p-1) ≡ 1 (mod p)`.
   - Here, `p = 13` (which is prime).
   - The base `a = 2`.
   - Since 13 does not divide 2, the conditions are met.

**Step 2: Apply Fermat's Little Theorem.**
   According to the theorem, `2^(13-1) ≡ 1 (mod 13)`, which means `2^12 ≡ 1 (mod 13)`.
   This tells us that the cycle of remainders for powers of 2 modulo 13 repeats every 12 steps, and specifically, the remainder is 1 when the exponent is a multiple of 12.

**Step 3: Reduce the exponent using the cycle length (p-1 = 12).**
   We need to find the remainder of the exponent 1000 when divided by 12.
   Divide 1000 by 12:
   1000 = 12 * 83 + 4 (Since 12 * 80 = 960, 12 * 3 = 36. 960 + 36 = 996. Remainder is 1000 - 996 = 4).
   So, `1000 ≡ 4 (mod 12)`.

**Step 4: Substitute the reduced exponent back into the congruence.**
   We can rewrite 2^1000 using the remainder found:
   `2^1000 = 2^(12 * 83 + 4) = (2^12)^83 * 2^4`

   Now apply the congruence `2^12 ≡ 1 (mod 13)`:
   `2^1000 ≡ (1)^83 * 2^4 (mod 13)`
   `2^1000 ≡ 1 * 2^4 (mod 13)`
   `2^1000 ≡ 2^4 (mod 13)`

**Step 5: Calculate the final remainder.**
   Calculate 2^4:
   2^4 = 16.
   Find the remainder of 16 when divided by 13:
   Rem(16 / 13) = 3.

   Therefore, `2^1000 ≡ 3 (mod 13)`. The remainder is 3.

This matches option (B).

## Shortcut/Tip

*   **FLT Trigger:** Recognize that a large exponent with a prime divisor is a strong indicator to use Fermat's Little Theorem. It significantly simplifies the problem compared to calculating the cyclicity manually (which would also give a cycle length of 12, but FLT finds it directly).
*   **Key Relation:** `a^k ≡ a^(k mod (p-1)) (mod p)` when FLT applies and `k` is not 0. (If `k mod (p-1)` is 0, then `a^k ≡ a^(p-1) ≡ 1 (mod p)`).
*   **Exponent Reduction:** The core step is reducing the large exponent (1000) by finding its remainder when divided by `p-1` (12). The problem boils down to calculating the remainder of a much smaller power (`2^4 mod 13`).
*   **Alternative - Cyclicity:** Manually finding the cycle: 2^1=2, 2^2=4, 2^3=8, 2^4=16->3, 2^5->6, 2^6->12->-1, 2^7->-2, 2^8->-4, 2^9->-8, 2^10->-16->-3, 2^11->-6, 2^12->-12->1. The cycle length is 12. This confirms FLT but takes longer. See [[01 - Number System/04.E03 - Remainder Cyclicity Example.md]] (Demonstrates the manual cyclicity method).
*   **Distractor Check:** (A) 1 is the remainder when the exponent is a multiple of 12. (C) 4 might come from 1000 mod 12 = 4, but forgetting to calculate 2^4 mod 13. (D) 9 is 3^2, perhaps confusing the base or remainder.
